## What is Injection Moulding?

Injection moulding is one of the most common manufacturing processes used to produce plastic components in large quantities. It is used to manufacture everything from bottle caps and toys to automotive parts, medical devices, and robot kit components.

In this process, plastic pellets are heated until they melt. The molten plastic is then injected into a precision-machined steel mould under very high pressure. After the plastic cools and solidifies, the mould opens and ejector pins push the finished component out. The cycle is then repeated, allowing thousands or even millions of identical parts to be produced with excellent accuracy.

## How Injection Moulding Works

### 1. Feeding Plastic Pellets

- Small plastic pellets (ABS, Polypropylene, Polycarbonate, Nylon, etc.) are loaded into a hopper.
- The pellets fall into a heated barrel where they are transported by a rotating screw.

### 2. Melting the Plastic

- Heaters and friction melt the plastic.
- The molten plastic reaches approximately 180°C–300°C depending on the material.
- The screw builds pressure and measures the required shot size.

### 3. Injection

- The screw moves forward like a plunger.
- Molten plastic is injected into the mould cavity at pressures exceeding 100 MPa (14,500 psi).

### 4. Cooling

- Cooling channels circulate water to remove heat.
- Cooling accounts for approximately 50–70% of the total cycle time.

### 5. Ejection

- The mould opens and ejector pins remove the finished part.
- Typical cycle time ranges from 10–60 seconds.

## Why Injection Moulding is Excellent for Mass Production

- One mould can produce thousands to millions of identical parts.
- Consistent dimensions and quality.
- High production speed with low labour.
- Very low cost per part at high production volumes.

## Everyday Examples

LEGO bricks, plastic bottle caps, water bottles, robot competition kit components, plastic gears, computer mouse shells, keyboard keys, mobile phone covers, plastic cups, electrical switch housings, automotive interior panels, storage boxes, toys.

## Important Design Rules

### 1. Draft Angles

Draft angles are small tapers (typically 1–3°) added to vertical walls to allow easy removal from the mould.

**Benefits**: Easier ejection, reduced mould wear, better surface finish, lower rejection rates.

### 2. Uniform Wall Thickness

Wall thickness should remain consistent throughout the part.

**Uneven wall thickness can cause**: Sink marks, warping, internal stresses.

Typical wall thickness is 1.5–3 mm for many consumer products.

### 3. Ribs

Ribs are thin reinforcing walls that increase stiffness without increasing wall thickness.

**Typical design guidelines**: Rib thickness 40–60% of wall thickness; rib height 2.5–3× wall thickness; include draft angles and fillets.

### 4. Weld Lines

Weld lines form where two plastic flow fronts meet. They are slightly weaker than surrounding material. Designers avoid placing highly loaded features such as screw holes on weld lines.

## Cost Structure

- High initial mould cost (hundreds to many thousands of US dollars).
- Low production cost per part.
- Ideal for mass production but not economical for very small production runs.

- Design stronger, lighter, and more manufacturable plastic parts.
